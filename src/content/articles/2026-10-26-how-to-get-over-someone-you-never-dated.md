To get over someone you never dated, treat it as a real loss, because it is one. Stop feeding the hope: no rereading old messages, no checking their stories. Let yourself grieve the future you imagined as well as the person. Then give that energy to people who give some back. Your brain doesn't care whether it was official.

Maybe it was a friend you fell for. A coworker who flirted every Thursday and then got a girlfriend. Someone you talked to for six weeks and never actually met. Whatever it was, there was no breakup, so nobody brought you ice cream. You're still crying in the car, though, and this guide is for that.

## Is It Normal to Grieve Someone You Never Dated?

Yes. Completely.

There's a name for grief that other people don't count as grief: disenfranchised grief. Grief researcher Kenneth Doka coined the term for losses that other people don't acknowledge or don't think matter ([Doka, 1989](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/psychology/disenfranchised-grief)). Losing someone you never officially had fits that well. You can't say "we broke up," so nobody gives you permission to fall apart.

The feelings aren't smaller for it. If anything they get heavier, since you're carrying them on your own.

Your body doesn't check the relationship status either. In one brain-imaging study, people who'd been through an unwanted breakup in the past six months looked at a photo of their ex, and areas that process physical pain responded ([Kross et al., 2011](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1102693108)). That's rejection, and it registers the same way after three years together or three almost-dates.

## Why Does It Hurt So Much When Nothing Happened?

Because a lot did happen, even if most of it happened in your head.

When you like someone who isn't fully available, you fill the gaps with imagination. You picture the first kiss, the holiday, how they'd get on with your friends. A real relationship eventually corrects the fantasy with real life: their bad moods, the dishes, the boring Sundays. An almost-relationship never does. So the person you're grieving is partly them and partly an edited version who never let you down.

There's also the uncertainty. If you didn't get a clear no, the door never quite shut. That "maybe" is the fuel for [limerence](/blog/articles/what-is-limerence/), the obsessive, can't-stop-thinking state that feeds on mixed signals.

And then there's the loneliness of it. Researchers who studied unrequited love from both sides, the would-be lover and the person who didn't return the feeling, described it in their title as a mix of "heartbreak, anger, guilt, scriptlessness, and humiliation" ([Baumeister, Wotman and Stillwell, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.64.3.377)). Scriptlessness might be the word you didn't know you needed. A breakup comes with rules. Unrequited love doesn't, and neither of you really knows how to act.

## Crush, Situationship or Unrequited Love: Does It Matter Which?

A little, because it changes what letting go looks like.

If it was a crush that never went anywhere, you may never have said a word. The hard part is the "what if," and most of the work happens in your own head.

With **unrequited love**, you said something (or it was obvious) and they don't feel the same. You have your answer, even if it came softly. The work is accepting it instead of hunting for a better one.

An **almost-relationship** is the one where you talked every day, maybe kissed, maybe more, and it never got a name. It overlaps with a situationship, and our guide on [how to get over a situationship](/blog/articles/how-to-get-over-a-situationship/) goes deeper. Treat it like any breakup. You need distance and you need time.

Falling for a friend is the cruelest version, because you can't easily step away. More on that below.

## How Do I Actually Let Go?

You can't switch the feeling off. What you can control is how much you feed it.

### Stop collecting evidence

Rereading old messages, checking who they follow, looking at their stories to see whether they're with someone. It feels like research, but all it does is keep the "maybe" alive. Mute them. Archive the chat. If you share a group chat, mute that too for a while. Our guide to [why you can't stop checking their Instagram](/blog/articles/stop-checking-ex-instagram/) covers the habit in detail.

### Grieve the future, out loud

Write down what you imagined. The trip, the inside jokes, meeting their family. Be specific. Yes, it hurts to write. It still beats having the fantasy run in the background all day. Once it's on paper, it's a loss you can be sad about, and eventually finish being sad about.

### Find someone who will take it seriously

Pick one friend and say plainly, "This is a real heartbreak for me, even though we never dated." Most people will meet you there if you ask. The ones who say "but you weren't even together" are not the right people for this one.

### Don't send the "just so you know" message

There's a pull to confess, re-confess or send one more message that explains everything. Before you do, ask what you're hoping they'll reply. If the honest answer is "that they'll suddenly feel the same," write it somewhere it can't send first. Our [unsent text tool](/tools/unsent-text/) is made for exactly this.

## What If We're Still Friends, or I See Them Every Day?

This is the hardest version. You can't go full no contact with a best friend, a classmate or the person two desks over.

What you can do is turn the volume down.

- Stop the one-on-one extras for now: the late-night chats, the "saw this and thought of you" memes, the long voice notes.
- Keep things friendly and short in person. Polite and present is enough.
- Don't ask about their dating life, and don't let mutual friends report on it.
- If it's a close friend, you can say something honest without reopening it: "I need a bit of space for a few weeks. It's not about anything you did."

A month of lower contact rarely ends a real friendship. Staying close while quietly hoping for months is much harder on it.

## How Long Does It Take to Get Over Someone You Never Dated?

There's no set number, and anyone who gives you one is guessing. What makes it shorter is the same as with any breakup: less contact, fewer reminders, more life outside the feeling.

What makes it longer is uncertainty. Every mixed signal resets the clock a little. So a clear answer, even a painful one, often does more for you than a kind maybe. If you can't get one, you can stop looking for one.

You'll know it's fading when you see their name pop up and your stomach doesn't drop. It might take a few weeks. It might take a season. Both are normal. ## FAQ: Getting Over Someone You Never Dated

**Why is it so hard to get over someone I never dated?**
Because you're grieving a future you imagined as well as the person, and that version never had a chance to disappoint you. Rejection also activates brain areas involved in physical pain, whether or not you were official.

**Is it OK to grieve a relationship that never happened?**
Yes. Grief that others don't recognize is called disenfranchised grief, and it's just as real. It often feels heavier because you don't get the usual support.

**Should I tell them how I feel to get closure?**
Only if you can accept any answer, including silence. If you're secretly hoping it changes their mind, write it out first somewhere it won't send and see how you feel the next day.

**How do I get over someone I see every day?**
Cut the extras rather than the person: stop late-night chats and personal messages, keep in-person contact friendly and brief, and avoid hearing about their dating life. A few weeks of lower contact gives the feeling room to fade.
