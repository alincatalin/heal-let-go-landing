A trauma bond is a strong emotional attachment to someone who hurts you, formed when harm and affection come in an unpredictable mix. The term comes from psychologists Donald Dutton and Susan Painter's research on abusive relationships. It isn't a clinical diagnosis, and online it's often used for breakups that were painful but not abusive.

## Where Does the Term "Trauma Bond" Come From?

Dutton and Painter developed the idea of traumatic bonding in the early 1980s, working with women who had been in abusive relationships. They pointed to two ingredients: a big imbalance of power, and treatment that swings between harm and warmth with no reliable pattern.

In a later study, they interviewed 75 women who had recently left abusive partners and followed up six months later. The women's attachment to the partner often persisted after they left, and the best predictors of it were how intermittent the abuse had been and shifts in the balance of power ([Dutton and Painter, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1891/0886-6708.8.2.105)).

## Why Does Intermittent Treatment Create Such a Strong Bond?

Think of the good days after a bad week: the apology, the version of them you fell for. When kindness is unpredictable, each return of it lands with huge relief, and you start to live for those moments.

Psychologists call this intermittent reinforcement, and it sits at the center of Dutton and Painter's theory: warmth that arrives unpredictably, after harm, can bind someone more tightly than steady kindness would. A breakup adds another layer. Brain scans of people recently rejected in love show activity in reward and craving regions also involved in addiction ([Fisher et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00784.2009)).

So the bond can feel like love. Some of it may be. A lot of it is a craving built on hurt followed by relief.

## Is Every Painful Breakup a Trauma Bond?

No. Online, the phrase now gets used for almost any ex you can't stop thinking about.

Missing someone badly, wanting them back, replaying the good times: those are normal after any breakup, including healthy relationships that ended. We explain that side in [why you miss your ex so much](/blog/articles/why-do-i-miss-my-ex/), and if you're stuck on someone you barely dated, [limerence](/blog/articles/what-is-limerence/) may fit better.

The term fits best when there was a real pattern of harm (control, cruelty, threats, violence, being made to feel small) mixed with warmth, and a power imbalance you couldn't easily push back against. A review of the research in a different setting, sex trafficking, described similar features: a power imbalance, a deliberate mix of good and bad treatment, gratitude for small kindnesses, and self-blame for the abuse ([Casassa, Knight and Mengo, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/1524838020985542)).

## How Does No Contact Help Break a Trauma Bond?

Every message, every "I'm sorry, I've changed," every late call restarts it. No contact removes the next round of relief, which is what keeps the hoping alive.

We should be honest about the evidence: that same review found no studies explaining how trauma bonds are broken. What we can say is that the cycle needs contact to keep going, and distance gives you room to see it. Expect the pull to last a while, though. In Dutton and Painter's study, attachment often outlasted the relationship. ## What If I'm Not Safe?

If the relationship was abusive, or you're afraid of your ex, please reach out for specialist support. In the US, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is free and confidential: call 1-800-799-7233, text START to 88788, or chat at [thehotline.org](https://www.thehotline.org). ## FAQ: Trauma Bonds

**Is a trauma bond a mental health diagnosis?**
No. It's a concept from research on abusive relationships, not a diagnosis in the DSM-5-TR or ICD-11. A therapist may still use it to describe what you're going through.

**How do I know if it's a trauma bond or just a hard breakup?**
A trauma bond involves a repeated pattern of harm mixed with affection, usually with a power imbalance. Missing an ex intensely, without that pattern, is a normal part of grief.

**How long does it take to break a trauma bond?**
There's no research-based timeline. Distance and support from people outside the relationship tend to help, and many people need a therapist or domestic abuse advocate for this.
