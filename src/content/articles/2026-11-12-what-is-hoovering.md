Weeks of silence. Then, out of nowhere: "I've been doing a lot of thinking. I miss you. I've changed."

If that message pulled you right back in, you might be getting hoovered.

## What Does Hoovering Mean?

Hoovering is named after the Hoover vacuum cleaner. It describes an ex trying to suck you back into the relationship, usually right when you've started to pull away or move on.

It isn't a clinical or scientific term. It comes from writing about abusive relationships and narcissistic behavior, where it describes a repeating cycle: harm, a breakup or silence, then a big effort to reel you back in. Today people also use it more loosely for any ex who keeps coming back.

## Signs of Hoovering

- **Sudden apologies and promises.** "I've changed." "I'm in therapy now." "It'll be different this time."
- **Nostalgia.** Old photos, "remember when", your song, an anniversary message.
- **A crisis.** They're ill, in trouble, or "have no one else to talk to".
- **Guilt.** "You promised you'd always be there." "I can't believe you'd just leave me like this."
- **Going through others.** Messages via friends or family, or reaching out to your relatives.
- **Gifts or showing up.** Flowers at your door, turning up at your work or your gym.
- **Timing.** It arrives when you start dating, post that you're happy, or go quiet for a while.

## Hoovering vs Breadcrumbing vs a Real Second Chance

| | What it looks like | What usually happens next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hoovering | A big push to get you back: apologies, promises, a crisis | Old patterns return once you're back |
| [Breadcrumbing](/blog/articles/what-is-breadcrumbing/) | Small, regular bits of attention, no real effort | Nothing changes, you keep waiting |
| Orbiting | Watching and liking your posts without talking | Keeps you guessing |
| A real second chance | Specific, lasting change, shown over time, at your pace | Respect for your answer, even if it's no |

The clearest test: **how do they react when you say no?** Someone who has really changed respects it. Hoovering usually escalates: more messages, more guilt, someone else asked to "talk sense into you".

Getting back together isn't rare. Breaking up and reuniting is common among young adults, but couples who go through that on-again, off-again cycle tend to report more conflict and less satisfaction than couples who don't ([Halpern-Meekin et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0743558412464524)).

## Why Is Hoovering So Hard to Resist?

Because it arrives when you're hurting, and it offers exactly what you've been wishing for. The apology, the "I miss you", proof that you mattered.

If the relationship swung between hurt and warmth, the pull can be even stronger. That pattern is at the center of what psychologists call [traumatic bonding](/blog/articles/what-is-a-trauma-bond/): warmth that comes back after harm can bind you more tightly than steady kindness would ([Dutton and Painter, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1891/0886-6708.8.2.105)).

## How to Respond to Hoovering

**1. Don't reply right away.** Give it at least a day. Hoovering works best on fast, emotional answers.

**2. Remember why it ended.** Write down the reasons now, while you're clear, and read them when the message comes.

**3. Decide whether to reply at all.** Silence is a full answer. If you do reply, keep it short: "I'm not going to get back together. Please don't contact me." Then stop.

**4. Don't argue or explain.** Each reason you give is something they can push back on.

**5. Block if it continues.** If they keep going after you've said no, block them. See [should I block my ex](/blog/articles/should-i-block-my-ex/).

**6. Tell someone.** Friends who know what happened can remind you when the promises sound convincing.

If your ex was abusive, threatening or controlling, hoovering can be a safety issue. Keep a record of messages, talk to someone you trust, and contact a domestic abuse service or find a local helpline at [findahelpline.com](https://findahelpline.com).

## FAQ: Hoovering

**Is hoovering always a narcissist thing?**
No. The term comes from writing about narcissistic and abusive relationships, but people use it for any ex who keeps trying to pull them back. You don't need to diagnose your ex to protect yourself.

**How do I know if my ex has really changed?**
Real change is specific and shows over time. They respect your boundaries, don't push when you say no, and don't need you back to prove it. Promises alone, especially early on, aren't evidence.

**Should I reply to a hoovering text?**
You don't have to. If you do, one short, clear message is enough. Then don't continue the conversation.

**Why does my ex come back when I start moving on?**
Because that's often when they notice you're no longer available. Your moving on is a sign it's working, not a reason to go back.
