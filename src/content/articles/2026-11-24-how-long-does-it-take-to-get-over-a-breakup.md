You want a number. Six weeks? Three months? Half the length of the relationship?

Here's the honest version: nobody can give you an exact date. But research on breakups tells us a lot about the shape of it, what the early weeks look like, and what makes the whole thing take longer than it has to. Most of that is in your hands.

## How Long Does It Take to Get Over a Breakup?

**Usually less time than you think it will.** In a study that followed college students through the first months of their relationships, the researchers caught 26 breakups as they happened. They asked people how upset they expected to be, then measured how upset they actually were at the breakup and two, six and ten weeks later. Distress dropped steadily over those ten weeks, and at every point people felt better than they had predicted they would ([Eastwick et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2007.07.001)). The overestimate was biggest for people who had been most in love.

That doesn't mean you'll be "over it" at week ten. It means the worst of it is front-loaded, and your brain is bad at believing that while you're in it.

A realistic picture for most people:

| Time since the breakup | What it often feels like | What helps most |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Days 1 to 7 | Shock, crying, no appetite or no sleep, checking your phone constantly | Get through each day. Tell people. Don't make big decisions |
| Weeks 2 to 4 | Craving for them, replaying the end, waves of hope that they'll come back | Cut contact and stop checking their profiles. Have one person you can call |
| Months 1 to 3 | More normal hours each day. Bad days still hit hard, often out of nowhere | Routines, sleep, seeing friends, doing things that are yours |
| Months 3 to 6 | They're on your mind sometimes, not all the time. Anniversaries and songs still sting | New plans. Notice what you don't miss |
| 6 months and beyond | Mostly a memory. You can think about them without your stomach dropping | If you're still stuck, talk to someone (see below) |

These ranges stretch for long relationships, marriages, living together, shared kids or a breakup you didn't see coming. They shrink when there's no contact and lots of support.

## Is There a Rule, Like Half the Length of the Relationship?

No. The "half the time you were together" rule is popular, but there's no research behind it. By that math, a six-year relationship would take three years to get over, and that isn't what most people experience.

What studies do show is that breakups hit hard across the board. In a US study of almost 1,300 unmarried adults followed for 20 months, people's distress went up and their life satisfaction went down after a breakup, compared with their own levels before it ([Rhoades et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023627)). The pain is real and measurable. It's also temporary for most people.

Length matters less than these things:

- **Whether you keep in contact.** Sadness and love for an ex fade more slowly for people who stay in touch ([Sbarra and Emery, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1350-4126.2005.00112.x)).
- **How much of your identity was "us".** People whose sense of who they are got blurrier after a breakup were more distressed ([Slotter et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167209352250)).
- **Your attachment style.** In a survey of more than 5,000 people, those high in attachment anxiety were more preoccupied with the ex, more distressed, and more likely to try to get back together ([Davis et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167203252884)).
- **Whether you saw it coming.** Being blindsided adds shock to the grief.

## What Makes It Take Longer

If it feels like you're not moving, it's usually one of these. They're common, and they're fixable.

**Staying in touch "as friends".** Every reply, like or "just checking in" restarts the clock. One study of separated adults found more contact with an ex was linked to more distress months later ([O'Hara et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/2167702620916454)). If you can't cut contact completely because of kids or work, keep it to logistics. Our [no contact rule guide](/blog/articles/no-contact-rule-guide/) covers how.

**Checking their Instagram.** People who kept looking at an ex's profile reported more distress, more longing and less personal growth ([Marshall, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1089/cyber.2012.0125)). Mute, archive, or block. Here's [how to stop checking their Instagram](/blog/articles/stop-checking-ex-instagram/).

**Replaying it on a loop.** Going over what went wrong again and again feels like working through it, but it keeps the wound open. Try the techniques in [how to stop thinking about your ex](/blog/articles/how-to-stop-thinking-about-your-ex/).

**Waiting for them to come back.** Hope is the hardest thing to let go of. As long as part of you is waiting, healing is on pause.

**Not sleeping, not eating, not moving.** Your body sets the pace for your mood. Nights are the worst for most people, which is why we wrote about [why breakups hurt more at night](/blog/articles/why-breakups-hurt-more-at-night/).

## What Helps You Get Over a Breakup Faster

You can't skip the grief. You can stop adding to it.

1. **Go no contact, or as close as you can.** It's the single biggest lever you control. If you want a number for how long, our free [how long should no contact last](/tools/how-long-no-contact/) calculator gives you one based on your situation.
2. **Write it out.** In a randomized study, students who wrote about their feelings about a breakup avoided the rise in tension, fatigue and physical symptoms that the comparison group had ([Lepore and Greenberg, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1080/08870440290025768)). A private journal or an [unsent letter](/blog/articles/unsent-letter-to-ex/) works.
3. **Be kind to yourself on purpose.** In a study of 109 divorcing adults, people who talked about the split with more self-compassion had less intrusive distress up to nine months later ([Sbarra et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611429466)). Talk to yourself like you'd talk to a friend.
4. **Rebuild the "me" part.** Do things that were yours before the relationship, or try something new. Recovering your sense of self came before feeling better in a study that followed students over two months after a breakup ([Mason et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2011.01378.x)).
5. **Lean on people.** Securely attached people in the large breakup survey turned to friends and family. Anxiously attached people were more likely to turn to alcohol or drugs ([Davis et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167203252884)). Text a friend instead of your ex.
6. **Count the days.** Progress is invisible from the inside. Seeing "day 23" on a screen is proof that you're moving, even on a day that feels like day 1. ## Signs You're Getting Over Your Ex

Healing rarely announces itself. You notice it in the gaps:

- You go a morning, then a whole day, without thinking about them.
- Their name on a screen makes you pause instead of panic.
- You stop checking their profiles, and you don't feel the itch.
- You can remember the bad parts as clearly as the good ones.
- You catch yourself making plans that have nothing to do with them.
- Hearing they're seeing someone hurts, but it doesn't flatten you.

If you can tick two or three of these, you're further along than you feel. For the no contact side, see [signs no contact is working](/blog/articles/signs-no-contact-is-working/).

## Why Does It Still Hurt After Months?

Some setbacks are normal. Anniversaries, holidays, a song in a shop, hearing they've moved on. A bad week at month four doesn't mean you're back at day one. It means something touched a bruise that's still healing.

It's also normal for the pain to be uneven. The [7 stages of a breakup](/blog/articles/7-stages-of-breakup/) aren't a straight line. Most people loop back to anger or sadness several times before acceptance sticks. Long relationships take longer too: our guide to [getting over a long-term relationship](/blog/articles/how-to-get-over-a-long-term-relationship/) covers why.

### When to get help

Talk to a doctor or therapist if, after a few weeks, you can't get through the day: you can't sleep or eat, can't work, or feel hopeless most of the time. Getting help early is not an overreaction. If you have any thoughts of harming yourself, contact a crisis line now. You can find one in your country at [findahelpline.com](https://findahelpline.com), or call or text 988 in the US.

## FAQ: How Long to Get Over a Breakup

**How long does it take to get over a breakup on average?**
There's no reliable average, because studies measure different things. What they agree on: the worst distress is usually in the first weeks, it declines steadily, and people recover faster than they expect. Many people feel a real shift within one to three months.

**How long does it take to get over a 1-year relationship?**
Often a few weeks to a few months. Shorter relationships can still hurt a lot, especially if you were very invested or it ended suddenly. How you handle contact matters more than the length.

**How long does it take to get over a long-term relationship?**
Usually longer, because your routines, friends and plans were shared. Many people feel noticeably better within a few months, with harder days around anniversaries for a year or more.

**Is it normal to not be over my ex after 6 months?**
Yes, especially after a long relationship or if you've been in and out of contact. If you're still not functioning day to day, it's worth talking to a professional. Otherwise, check whether contact, social media or waiting for them is keeping it fresh.

**Do guys get over breakups faster?**
Not reliably. In a large survey, men and women were equally likely to be anxious or avoidant after a breakup, though women reported more anger and more emotional involvement before the breakup ([Davis et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167203252884)). How someone looks on Instagram tells you little about how they're doing.

**Does no contact make it faster?**
It helps. Contact keeps sadness and longing going for longer. Use our free [no contact counter](/tools/no-contact-counter/) to see what day you're on and what usually comes next.

**Why do I feel worse a few weeks in?**
The shock wears off and reality sets in. Many people find weeks two to four harder than the first days. Read [when does no contact get easier](/blog/articles/when-does-no-contact-get-easier/) for what usually comes next.
