You were fine for an hour. Then a song, a street, a smell, and they're back in your head, along with the same five conversations you've already replayed a hundred times.

If you're searching how to stop thinking about your ex, you've probably already tried "just don't." Here's why that doesn't work, and what does.

## Can You Actually Stop Thinking About Your Ex?

**Not on command, but you can make the thoughts come less often and hurt less when they do.** The goal isn't a blank mind by Friday. It's going from "all day" to "sometimes", and from "it wrecks my evening" to "that was sad, and then I made dinner".

That happens through three things, in this order:

1. **Stop feeding the thoughts** (contact, their profile, old chats).
2. **Change how you respond** when a thought shows up, instead of fighting it.
3. **Give it time.** Distance does most of the work, but only if steps 1 and 2 give it a chance.

## Why Can't I Stop Thinking About My Ex?

Because your brain learned to expect them. They were in your day, your plans and your phone, and that habit doesn't end when the relationship does.

There's also a craving side to it. In a brain-scan study of people who had just been rejected by a partner, looking at a photo of the ex activated reward and craving areas linked to addiction ([Fisher et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00784.2009)). Wanting someone doesn't stop because they're gone. With no new contact, your mind replays old material instead.

Three things make the loop louder:

- **Unanswered questions.** "Why did they do that?" "Do they miss me?" Your brain keeps reopening a file that can't be closed from your side.
- **Fresh fuel.** Every look at their stories, every reread of the chat, tops the craving up.
- **The highlight reel.** Memory keeps the good moments and softens the bad ones, so the person you think about is an edited version. (More on that in [why do I miss my ex so much](/blog/articles/why-do-i-miss-my-ex/).)

## Why "Just Don't Think About Them" Backfires

In a classic experiment, people were asked not to think about a white bear for five minutes and to ring a bell every time they did. Then they were allowed to think about it. Those who had tried to suppress the thought ended up thinking about the bear more afterwards than people who hadn't ([Wegner et al., 1987](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.53.1.5)). Psychologists call it the rebound effect.

Your ex is a much bigger white bear. Telling yourself "stop thinking about them" puts them right back in focus. So the techniques below don't try to block the thought. They give it somewhere else to go.

## What Actually Works: 6 Strategies Compared

| Strategy | What you do | What it does | Use it when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cut the fuel** | Mute or block them, archive the chat, move photos out of your camera roll | People who kept checking an ex's Facebook page reported more distress and longing and less growth ([Marshall, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1089/cyber.2012.0125)) | First, before anything else |
| **Remind yourself why it ended** | Write 3-5 real things that didn't work and read them when you idealize them | In a lab study, thinking about an ex's negative sides was the only strategy that lowered love feelings, though it made people feel worse in the moment ([Langeslag and Sanchez, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000360)) | When the highlight reel starts |
| **Switch to something absorbing** | A call, a walk, a show, cooking, a game: something that takes your full attention | In the same study, thinking about something pleasant and unrelated lifted mood but didn't change love feelings | When a thought hits and you need to feel better now |
| **Give thoughts a time slot** | 15-20 minutes a day, same time, to think, cry, replay. Outside it: "not now, at 8pm." | Contains the thinking instead of fighting it. A common technique, not a cure | When the thoughts are all day |
| **Write it out once** | Write everything you'd say to them, somewhere it can't send | Gets it out of your head. But going over and over why it happened can keep you stuck ([Sbarra et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/2167702612469801)), so write to let it out, not to solve it | When a message is building up |
| **Talk to someone** | A friend, a therapist, or a coach that's there at 2am | Saying it out loud turns a loop into a conversation | When you notice the same thoughts circling |

The combination matters. Reminding yourself of the downsides loosens the attachment but feels bad. Doing something you enjoy feels good but doesn't loosen it. Using both, one after the other, may give you some of each. As the researcher behind that study put it, love regulation isn't an on/off switch: you have to keep doing it.

## A 10-Minute Plan for When Your Ex Pops Into Your Head

Save this for the moment it happens:

1. **Name it.** "I'm thinking about them again." Naming it can put a little space between you and the thought.
2. **Don't argue with it.** You're not trying to make it go away. Let it be there.
3. **Read one line from your list** of why it ended. Just one.
4. **Then switch.** Do one absorbing thing for ten minutes. Not scrolling: something that needs your hands or your full attention.
5. **If it's still loud after ten minutes, write it down** and save it for your thinking slot.

Most of the time the wave passes inside those ten minutes. It comes back, and it passes again, and each time it's a little smaller.

## How to Stop Thinking About Your Ex at Night

Nights are when it's hardest. You're tired, it's quiet, and there's nothing to distract you. Feelings run hotter late at night, which is why we wrote a whole guide on [why breakups hurt more at night](/blog/articles/why-breakups-hurt-more-at-night/).

What helps:

- **Phone out of reach.** Charge it across the room. Most "just one look" checks happen in bed.
- **Have a fixed wind-down.** The same podcast, audiobook or playlist each night, so your mind has somewhere to rest that isn't them.
- **Keep a notepad by the bed.** When a thought won't leave, write it down and tell yourself you'll deal with it tomorrow.

## Cut the Triggers: A Checklist

You don't have to delete your past. You have to stop bumping into it ten times a day.

- Mute or unfollow them on every app (or [block them](/blog/articles/should-i-block-my-ex/) if muting isn't enough)
- Archive the chat so it's not on your home screen
- Move photos into a hidden album
- Take "your" playlists off shuffle for now
- Put their things in a box out of sight ([what to do with your ex's stuff](/blog/articles/what-to-do-with-your-exs-stuff/))
- Ask friends not to update you on them
- Stop searching their name, their new partner or their friends' profiles ([how to stop checking their Instagram](/blog/articles/stop-checking-ex-instagram/))

## How Long Until I Stop Thinking About My Ex?

There's no fixed number, and anyone who gives you one is guessing. It depends on how long you were together, how it ended, and above all on whether you're still in contact or still checking on them.

What usually happens: the thoughts go from constant, to daily, to "sometimes, when something reminds me". In a study that followed young adults after a breakup, sadness and love for the ex faded over the following weeks, and more slowly for people who stayed in contact ([Sbarra and Emery, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1350-4126.2005.00112.x)). That's why [no contact](/blog/articles/no-contact-rule-guide/) matters so much here. For what the stages look like, see [the 7 stages of a breakup](/blog/articles/7-stages-of-breakup/).

## When It's More Than Missing Them

Sometimes the thoughts are a sign of something bigger:

- If the person is the center of your thoughts and your mood rises and falls with any sign from them, read about [limerence](/blog/articles/what-is-limerence/).
- If you keep going back to someone who hurt you, read about [trauma bonds](/blog/articles/what-is-a-trauma-bond/).
- If you can't sleep, eat or work for weeks, or you have thoughts of harming yourself, please talk to a doctor or therapist, or find a free helpline near you at [findahelpline.com](https://findahelpline.com). That's not weakness. It's what support is for.

## FAQ: How to Stop Thinking About Your Ex

**How do I stop thinking about my ex all the time?**
Stop feeding the thoughts first: no checking their profile, no rereading old chats. Then give the thoughts a set time each day, and when they come outside it, switch to something that takes your full attention. Over weeks, "all the time" becomes "sometimes".

**Why do I keep thinking about my ex after months?**
Usually because something still feeds it: you're checking on them, you're still in contact, or there are questions you haven't let go of. Thoughts also come back around birthdays, holidays and when you hear news about them. A return isn't a relapse. It usually passes faster each time.

**Is it normal to think about your ex every day?**
Yes, especially in the first weeks and months. It's what happens when someone who was part of every day isn't anymore. If it's still every hour after a few months, or it's affecting sleep and work, talking to someone can help.

**How do I stop thinking about my ex at night?**
Keep your phone out of reach, use the same wind-down each night, and keep a notepad by the bed for thoughts that won't leave. If you need to talk, use something that can't reach them, like a journal or Heal's coach.

**Does thinking about my ex mean I still love them?**
Not necessarily. You can think about someone out of habit, hurt, unanswered questions or loneliness. Thinking about them is part of how your mind processes the loss.

**Should I let myself think about my ex?**
Yes, within limits. Fighting the thoughts tends to make them come back stronger. Giving them a set time each day, and then getting on with the rest of it, works better than trying to block them out.
