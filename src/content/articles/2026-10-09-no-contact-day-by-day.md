You're probably here because you're on a specific day and want to know if what you're feeling is normal. Short answer: almost certainly yes.

This page walks through the first 30 days of no contact one day at a time: what people commonly feel, and one thing that helps. Some days have their own full guide, linked below.

Two caveats before you scroll to your day:

*   **Nobody's month follows this exactly.** Your worst day might be day 2 or day 9. Treat this as a map of what's common, not a schedule you're failing.
*   **A bad day doesn't reset your progress.** Feeling like day 3 again on day 12 is part of the shape, not a return to the start.

## The First Week: Withdrawal

The first seven days are usually the most physical. Your brain is adjusting to the absence of someone it's used to hearing from, and it pushes back.

### Day 1

The decision is fresh. You might feel determined, numb or sick, or all three by the evening. Today matters less for how you feel and more for how you set up: mute the chat, write your reasons list, and tell one friend what you're doing. **Full guide: [No contact day 1](/blog/articles/no-contact-day-1/).**

### Day 2

The adrenaline from deciding starts to fade, and the habit of reaching for your phone gets noticeable. You'll catch yourself opening their chat without meaning to. **What helps:** move the app off your home screen. Small friction beats willpower.

### Day 3

For many people, this is the peak: the craving is loud, and your brain is good at producing reasons why one message wouldn't hurt. Evenings and late nights are the danger zone. **Full guide: [Why day 3 is the hardest](/blog/articles/hardest-day-no-contact/).**

### Day 4

If day 3 was the peak, day 4 can feel like a hangover: tired, flat, emotionally sore. Some people feel a little lighter; others feel worse because the hope that it would pass quickly didn't come true. **What helps:** lower the bar for today. Eat, sleep, get outside once. That counts.

### Day 5

The urge is often quieter, but rumination is louder: replaying conversations, rewriting arguments, imagining what they're doing. **What helps:** give the loop an exit. Write the conversation you keep replaying, in full, somewhere it can't send. Then close it.

### Day 6

The first weekend often lands around now, and weekends are hard when they used to be shared. Unstructured hours invite the "just check" impulse. **What helps:** plan the weekend before it starts, with at least one thing involving another person.

### Day 7

One week. The acute edge often starts to ease, and a quieter set of traps shows up, like "I'm doing better, so one message is fine." **Full guide: [No contact day 7](/blog/articles/no-contact-day-7/).**

## The Second Week: The Quiet Gets Loud

With less energy going into resisting the urge, there's more room for grief, loneliness and doubt.

### Day 8

Starting a second week can feel like the reward should have arrived by now, and it hasn't. That disappointment is normal. **What helps:** look at what's already different from day 1, not at what still hurts.

### Day 9

Many people notice their first real gaps: an hour, maybe a morning, where they didn't think about them. Then guilt or sadness about not thinking about them. **What helps:** let the gaps happen without analyzing them. They're the point.

### Day 10

Double digits. Day 10 is a common time for "maybe I overreacted" thinking, as memory starts to soften the hard parts. **What helps:** reread your reasons list, slowly, and add anything you'd started to forget.

### Day 11

Loneliness tends to show up more clearly in week two, especially in the hours you used to spend together. **What helps:** fill one of those exact hours with someone else this week: a call, a walk, a meal.

### Day 12

Day 12 is often when checking replaces texting as the main temptation: their profile, their stories, mutual friends' posts. It feels harmless because they won't know. **What helps:** read [how to stop checking their Instagram](/blog/articles/stop-checking-ex-instagram/), and mute what you can.

### Day 13

Expect an uneven day. People often describe week two as a string of okay days interrupted by one bad one out of nowhere. **What helps:** when the bad one comes, name it for what it is ("a bad day, not a reset") and get through to tomorrow.

### Day 14

Two weeks. The craving is usually quieter, but memories, doubt and "what ifs" can be louder than in week one. **Full guide: [No contact day 14](/blog/articles/no-contact-day-14/).**

## The Third Week: Steadier, With Dips

For many people, week three brings longer stretches of normal, which makes the remaining dips feel sharper by contrast.

### Day 15

Halfway through the month. You may notice you've stopped counting hours and started counting days. **What helps:** pick one routine the relationship crowded out and put it back in your week on purpose.

### Day 16

Triggers become more specific: a song, a street, a smell, a date on the calendar. They hit hard but pass faster than they used to. **What helps:** when one hits, notice what set it off. Knowing your triggers lets you plan around them.

### Day 17

Restlessness or boredom can appear now, a sign that energy is coming back with nowhere to go yet. **What helps:** start something small that's yours: a class, a project, a habit, a trip you'd been putting off.

### Day 18

A common day for curiosity about them: not frantic, just "I wonder how they are." It's quieter than the craving of day 3, but it can lead to the same place. **What helps:** let the question stay unanswered. You don't need the answer to keep healing.

### Day 19

You might be seeing the relationship more clearly: patterns you couldn't see while you were in it. **What helps:** write down what you've learned about what you need and what you won't accept next time.

### Day 20

Watch for numbing: more drinking, endless scrolling, rushing into dating to prove a point. None is wrong on its own; the question is whether you're choosing it or using it to avoid feeling. **What helps:** one honest check-in with yourself before the evening starts.

### Day 21

Three weeks. Life feels more normal, which can make the moments you still miss them hit harder. **Full guide: [No contact day 21](/blog/articles/no-contact-day-21/).**

## The Fourth Week: Toward Day 30

The last stretch before the one-month mark is less about surviving and more about deciding who you are without them.

### Day 22

Some people feel "over it" here and start to wonder whether no contact is still necessary. That feeling is real progress, but it's newer than it feels. **What helps:** decide now that you'll make no decisions about contact before day 30.

### Day 23

A dip often arrives in week four, sometimes triggered by news about them. It can feel like going backwards. **What helps:** compare today's worst moment with day 3's. The difference is your progress.

### Day 24

Your energy is likely going into your own life again: work, friends, plans. **What helps:** notice it, and say it out loud to someone. Progress you name is easier to believe.

### Day 25

The "friendly" check-in starts to sound reasonable: wishing them well, asking how they are. If you'd be hoping for something in their reply, it isn't friendly yet. **What helps:** our guide on [whether to text your ex](/blog/articles/should-i-text-my-ex/) has a test for telling the difference.

### Day 26

A good day to look back at what you wrote on day 1. It often reads like someone else wrote it. **What helps:** add a line to your reasons list about how you feel now.

### Day 27

Approaching the milestone can bring pressure, as if day 30 should arrive with a feeling of being finished. It usually doesn't, and that's fine. **What helps:** let day 30 be a checkpoint, not a finish line.

### Day 28

Four weeks. If you have a set no-contact length in mind, now's the time to decide whether to extend it, calmly and in daylight. **What helps:** read [how long no contact should last](/blog/articles/no-contact-rule-guide/) in our full guide.

### Day 29

The eve of a month. Some people feel the urge to "mark" it by reaching out. Mark it for yourself instead: a meal, a walk somewhere new, a small treat. **What helps:** plan that now.

### Day 30

One month. Usually not the end of missing them, but often the first point where you're clear-headed enough to decide what comes next. **Full guide: [No contact day 30](/blog/articles/no-contact-day-30/).**

## "What Is He Thinking on Day ___ of No Contact?"

It's one of the most common things people search alongside a day number, so here's the honest answer for every day on this page: you can't know. People react to breakups very differently. Some feel relief, some regret, many feel both. Their silence, or a message from them, tells you very little about what they feel.

What you can know is what you're thinking. If the question keeps looping, ask what you hope the answer is and what you'd do with it. Usually it's "they miss me, so maybe I should reach out," which is the urge in a more reasonable-sounding form. More on this in [what is he thinking during no contact](/blog/articles/what-is-he-thinking-during-no-contact/).

## If You Broke No Contact

Then today is day 1 again, but you're not starting from zero. You know your danger hours, what the urge feels like, and that it passes. Use the [day 1 setup](/blog/articles/no-contact-day-1/) to close whatever gap let you slip, and keep going.

## FAQ: No Contact Day by Day

**What is the hardest day of no contact?**
For many people it's around day 3, when the initial adrenaline is gone and the craving peaks. But the hardest day varies: plenty of people find a day in week two harder because loneliness and doubt take over.

**When does no contact start to get easier?**
Often somewhere between day 5 and day 10 the urges become less frequent and shorter, though bad days still come. By weeks three and four, most people report more good days than bad.

**How long should no contact last?**
Thirty days is the most common minimum. Many people extend to 60 or 90 days, especially if they still feel a strong pull toward their ex. Our [no contact rule guide](/blog/articles/no-contact-rule-guide/) covers how to decide.

**Does a bad day mean no contact isn't working?**
No. Healing isn't linear, and an unexpectedly hard day in week two or three is very common. What matters is that the bad moments pass faster than they used to.

If at any point you're struggling to eat, sleep or function, or the low mood isn't lifting at all, please talk to a doctor or therapist. Some breakups need more support than a plan can give.
