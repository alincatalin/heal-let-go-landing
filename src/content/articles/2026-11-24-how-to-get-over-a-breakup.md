Everyone tells you that time heals. What nobody tells you is what to do with the time.

This guide is the "what to do". Ten steps, in roughly the order they matter, with what research says about each and what to skip. It works whoever ended it and however long you were together.

## How Do You Get Over a Breakup?

**You get over a breakup by giving the pain less fuel and your life more of it.** Grief has to run its course, and you can't skip it. But most of what keeps people stuck is something they're still doing: talking to the ex, watching their stories, replaying the end, waiting.

The short version:

1. Go no contact (or as close as you can)
2. Remove the reminders from your phone
3. Let yourself feel it, on a schedule
4. Write it out, never send it
5. Tell your people, and let them help
6. Look after your body first
7. Remind yourself why it ended
8. Build days that don't revolve around them
9. Rebuild the "me" that got folded into "us"
10. Count your progress, because you won't feel it

The rest of this guide explains each one.

## 1. Go No Contact, or as Close as You Can

Contact is the biggest thing that slows healing down. In a study of young adults after a breakup, sadness and love for the ex faded more slowly for people who stayed in touch ([Sbarra and Emery, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1350-4126.2005.00112.x)).

No contact means no texts, calls, likes, replies to stories or "happy birthday". If you share kids, a job or a lease, keep it to logistics, short and polite. Our [no contact rule guide](/blog/articles/no-contact-rule-guide/) covers the details, and [what counts as breaking no contact](/blog/articles/what-counts-as-breaking-no-contact/) answers the grey areas.

Your brain is going to fight this. Rejection lights up the same reward and craving areas involved in addiction ([Fisher et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00784.2009)), so the urge to reach out is strong and physical. That's normal, and it passes faster every time you don't act on it.

## 2. Remove the Reminders From Your Phone

Your phone is where your ex lives now. Mute or unfollow them on every app, archive the chat, and move photos into a hidden album. You don't have to delete anything forever.

This matters more than it sounds. People who kept looking at an ex's Facebook page reported more distress, more longing and less personal growth than people who didn't ([Marshall, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1089/cyber.2012.0125)). If you can't stop checking, read [how to stop checking your ex's Instagram](/blog/articles/stop-checking-ex-instagram/) or consider whether to [block your ex](/blog/articles/should-i-block-my-ex/).

## 3. Let Yourself Feel It, on a Schedule

Pushing the feelings away backfires. In a classic experiment, people who tried not to think about something ended up thinking about it more afterwards ([Wegner et al., 1987](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.53.1.5)).

Instead, give the grief a slot. Twenty minutes in the evening to cry, listen to the sad songs, look at what you need to look at. When it shows up outside that slot, tell yourself "later", and mean it. You'll find it gets smaller when it knows it has a place to go. Our guide on [how to stop thinking about your ex](/blog/articles/how-to-stop-thinking-about-your-ex/) has more techniques like this.

## 4. Write It Out, Never Send It

Writing about a breakup does more than make you feel heard. In a randomized study, students who wrote about their feelings after a breakup avoided the rise in tension, fatigue and physical symptoms that a comparison group had ([Lepore and Greenberg, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1080/08870440290025768)).

Write the angry version, the begging version, the "I still love you" version. Then don't send any of it. An [unsent letter to your ex](/blog/articles/unsent-letter-to-ex/) is a good format, and our free [unsent text tool](/tools/unsent-text/) lets you type it in a chat that never delivers. Write to let it out, not to solve the relationship: going over why it happened again and again can keep some people stuck ([Sbarra et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/2167702612469801)).

## 5. Tell Your People, and Let Them Help

You don't need a support group. You need two or three people who know what happened and who you can text instead of your ex.

In a survey of more than 5,000 people after a breakup, those who coped well tended to use friends and family as a safe place. People who were more anxious about relationships were more likely to turn to drinking or drugs ([Davis et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167203252884)).

Make it concrete. Ask one friend: "If I text you 'about to do something stupid', can you call me?" That's a real plan.

## 6. Look After Your Body First

Breakups hurt physically. In a brain-scan study, looking at a photo of an ex who had broken up with you activated some of the same regions as physical pain ([Kross et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1102693108)). Your body is in it too.

Before you work on your mindset, cover the basics:

- **Sleep:** same bedtime, phone out of the bedroom, or at least out of reach. Nights are the hardest part for most people. Here's [why breakups hurt more at night](/blog/articles/why-breakups-hurt-more-at-night/).
- **Food:** something small and regular, even if you're not hungry.
- **Movement:** a 20-minute walk counts. Outside is better.
- **Alcohol:** go easy. It makes 1am texts far more likely and next-day mood worse.

## 7. Remind Yourself Why It Ended

After a breakup, memory turns into a highlight reel. You remember the best weekend, not the fights.

In a lab study, the one strategy that actually reduced people's love feelings for an ex was thinking about the ex's negative sides. It made people feel a little worse in the moment, while distraction made them feel better but didn't loosen the attachment ([Langeslag and Sanchez, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000360)).

Write a list of five real things that didn't work: specific moments, not insults. Read it when you start idealizing them. Our free [reasons not to text them](/tools/reasons-not-to-text/) tool turns the list into a lock screen, so it's the first thing you see when you reach for your phone.

## 8. Build Days That Don't Revolve Around Them

Empty time is when the pull is strongest: Sunday afternoons, the commute, the hour before bed. Plan those on purpose.

Put two or three small things in your week that are easy to start: a class, a standing call with a friend, the gym at the same time, a walk with a podcast. Not to "stay busy", but so there's a shape to the days that doesn't have them in it.

## 9. Rebuild the "Me" That Got Folded Into "Us"

Part of what hurts is losing a version of yourself. Researchers found that after a breakup, people's sense of who they are gets less clear, and that loss of clarity predicted more distress ([Slotter et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167209352250)). In another study, people whose sense of self recovered first went on to feel better ([Mason et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2011.01378.x)).

Ask yourself what you used to do before them, what you gave up for the relationship, and what you've wanted to try. Then do one of those things this week. Be kind to yourself while you do it: in a study of divorcing adults, people who talked about the split with more self-compassion recovered better over the following months ([Sbarra et al., 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611429466)).

## 10. Count Your Progress, Because You Won't Feel It

Healing is slow enough that you can't see it day to day. On a bad Tuesday at week five, it feels like nothing has changed. Something has.

## What Not to Do After a Breakup

| What people do | Why it feels right | What usually happens | Try this instead |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Text "just to talk" | It eases the ache for a minute | The ache comes back stronger, and you wait for a reply | Write it in an unsent note |
| Stay friends right away | You don't want to lose them completely | You stay on hold, hoping | Take months of distance first. See [can you be friends with your ex](/blog/articles/can-you-be-friends-with-your-ex/) |
| Check their stories | You want to know if they're okay, or sad | Each check restarts the longing | Mute them everywhere |
| Ask mutual friends about them | It feels like staying close | You get fragments that feed rumination | Ask friends not to bring them up |
| Jump into someone new to forget | It distracts and boosts your ego | Can help some people, but not if it's only about them | Wait until you want the new person, not the distraction |
| Drink to sleep | It knocks you out | Worse sleep, worse mood, riskier texts | Wind-down routine, phone across the room |
| Wait for them to come back | Hope feels better than grief | Healing stays on pause | Decide you're moving on, whatever they do |

## How to Get Over a Breakup You Didn't Want

When you didn't choose it, there's an extra layer: rejection and the feeling of being powerless. Two things help.

First, take the decision back. They decided to end the relationship, but you decide whether you keep waiting. Choosing no contact is choosing to stop waiting.

Second, don't try to understand it fully before you move on. "Why?" can take months to answer, and some of the answer may only make sense later. You can heal without it. [How to get over him](/blog/articles/how-to-get-over-him/) has a 30-day plan for the first month after being left.

## How to Get Over a Breakup When You Still Love Them

You don't need to stop loving them to move on. Love fades with distance and time, not by force. What you can change is what you do with it: not feeding it with contact and scrolling, not acting on it at 1am.

If you're worried you're the one who made a mistake, give it time before you act. Our pillar guide on [whether you should text your ex](/blog/articles/should-i-text-my-ex/) and the quick [should I text my ex quiz](/tools/should-i-text-my-ex/) help you decide with a clear head.

## How Long Does It Take?

There's no fixed number, but it's usually shorter than it feels. In one study, distress after a breakup fell steadily over ten weeks and was lower than people predicted it would be ([Eastwick et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2007.07.001)). Our [breakup recovery timeline](/blog/articles/how-long-does-it-take-to-get-over-a-breakup/) goes week by week, and the [7 stages of a breakup](/blog/articles/7-stages-of-breakup/) explains why it doesn't feel like a straight line.

If you're not functioning after a few weeks (not sleeping, not eating, not working, or feeling hopeless), talk to a doctor or therapist. If you have any thoughts of harming yourself, contact a crisis line now: [findahelpline.com](https://findahelpline.com) lists one for your country, and in the US you can call or text 988.

## FAQ: How to Get Over a Breakup

**What is the fastest way to get over a breakup?**
There's no shortcut through the grief, but there is a way to stop making it longer: no contact, no checking their profiles, and lots of support. Those three cut out most of what keeps people stuck.

**What should I do the first week after a breakup?**
Survive it. Tell two friends, mute your ex everywhere, eat and sleep as well as you can, and don't make big decisions. Our [day 1 of no contact](/blog/articles/no-contact-day-1/) guide walks through the first days.

**Is it normal to feel physically sick after a breakup?**
Yes. Brain-scan research shows a painful breakup overlaps with physical pain processing. Loss of appetite, a tight chest and poor sleep are common in the first weeks. See a doctor if they don't ease.

**Should I get rid of my ex's things?**
Not straight away. Put them in a box, out of sight. Decide what to keep, return or throw out once the first weeks have passed. Here's [what to do with your ex's stuff](/blog/articles/what-to-do-with-your-exs-stuff/).

**How do I stop missing my ex?**
Missing them is mostly a craving, and it fades the less you feed it. Read [why do I miss my ex](/blog/articles/why-do-i-miss-my-ex/) for what's going on and what helps.

**Can a breakup make you a better person?**
It can. Many people find that, once the worst is over, they know more clearly what they want and don't want. That comes later, though. In the first weeks, getting through the day is enough.

**What if I can't stop crying?**
Cry. It's not a sign you're doing it wrong. Give it a time and a place, and if you're crying all day after several weeks, talk to someone.
