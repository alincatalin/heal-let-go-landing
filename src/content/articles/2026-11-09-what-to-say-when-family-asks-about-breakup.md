You can picture it already. Plates going around, someone's uncle carving, and then, from across the table: "So where's Josh this year?"

Below are lines you can steal, sorted by who's asking and how it ended. Screenshot the ones you need.

## What Should I Say When Someone Asks "Where's Josh?"

The basic shape is the same for everyone: answer, close, redirect.

> "Josh and I broke up. I'm okay. Did you end up getting the new car?"

> "We're not together anymore. I'd rather not go into it today, but thanks for asking."

> "It ended in August. Can you pass the stuffing?"

The redirect matters most. People usually ask because they don't know what else to say, and they're relieved when you hand them a new topic.

## What Do I Tell My Parents?

Parents get a little more, ideally by phone before the big day, so the dinner table isn't where they find out.

> "I wanted to tell you before Thanksgiving: Josh and I broke up. I'm sad, but I'm okay. I don't want to talk about it much at dinner. Could you help me keep it low-key?"

That last line turns them into allies. If your mom is the type who'll tell everyone anyway, ask her to: "Can you let the aunts know so I don't have to say it ten times?"

## What About Grandparents?

Short and warm. Grandparents mostly want to know you're looked after.

> "We're not together anymore, Grandma. I'm doing fine, and I've got good people around me."

If they say "You'll find someone better," you can just say "I hope so" and squeeze their hand. You don't need to explain modern dating at the table.

## How Do I Handle the Nosy Aunt?

She will ask a second question. And a third. Decide now that you'll repeat yourself instead of explaining more. It's kinder than a speech, and it works.

> "It just didn't work out."

> "Like I said, it didn't work out. Anyway, how's the kitchen remodel?"

If she keeps going:

> "I know you care. I'm not going to talk about it today."

Then stand up and refill something.

## What Do I Say to Siblings, Kids and Family Friends?

**Siblings** can usually get the real version, earlier, in private. Ask one to run interference: "If Aunt Carol starts on Josh, can you jump in with something about your kids?"

**Kids** (nieces, nephews, a friend's six-year-old) ask the most direct questions and need the simplest answers:

> "Josh isn't coming anymore. We're not boyfriend and girlfriend now. Want to show me your drawing?"

**Family friends** and neighbors at the open house get the shortest line of all:

> "We broke up, actually. How have you been?"

## What If a Coworker Asks at the Holiday Party?

Work is the place to say the least. You'll see these people on Monday.

> "We split up a while ago. I'm good. Are you traveling for the holidays?"

You don't have to say it was recent, or who ended it. "A while ago" can mean three weeks.

## Does It Change Depending on How We Broke Up?

A little. Here are lines for the most common situations.

- **It's fresh (days or weeks):** "It's very new and I'm not ready to talk about it. I'll fill you in another time."
- **You were dumped:** "He ended it. I'm sad, but I'll be okay." You don't have to pretend it was mutual, and you don't have to explain why.
- **You ended it:** "I ended it. It was the right call, even if it's hard." If someone says "But he was so nice," try: "He is nice. We weren't right."
- **They want you to get back together:** "I know you liked us together. We're not getting back together, and it helps me most when people don't suggest it."
- **They really liked him:** "I know you liked him. You're allowed to miss him too. I just can't be the one you talk to about it right now."

Your family might be a little sad too. You don't have to manage their feelings about him on top of your own.

## How Do I Set a Boundary Without Starting a Fight?

Ask for what would help. "Please stop asking" can sound like an accusation. This lands softer:

> "I love that you care. What would help me most today is not talking about Josh. Can we talk about literally anything else?"

Say it once, kindly. Next time, just "Not today." If someone keeps pushing after that, you can leave the room, or leave early. Driving yourself makes that much easier.

## What If I Cry at the Table?

It happens. Someone mentions last year's photos, or plays the song, and your eyes go.

You can just say:

> "Sorry, it's still a bit raw. Give me a minute."

Then go to the bathroom, run cold water over your wrists, and breathe out slowly a few times. Come back when you're ready, or don't. Most families have seen worse over a turkey.

## What If My Family Still Talks to My Ex?

This is where [no contact](/blog/articles/no-contact-rule-guide/) quietly breaks. Your cousin still plays basketball with Josh. Your mom still likes his posts. And then, over pie: "Oh, Josh's mom said he's seeing someone."

News about him is contact with him by another route. In a study that followed young adults for four weeks after a breakup, people who stayed in contact with their ex saw their love and sadness fade more slowly ([Sbarra and Emery, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1350-4126.2005.00112.x)). That study was about direct contact, but a steady drip of updates keeps him in the room in a similar way.

Ask, ahead of time, for a news blackout:

> "I know you're still in touch with Josh, and that's okay. Please don't pass on anything about him, good or bad. Not even if I ask."

The "not even if I ask" part is for the version of you at 11pm on day nine. And if news slips out anyway:

> "I'd rather not know. Thanks."

If he still follows your siblings or your mom online, you can't control that. You can mute him on your own accounts, and mute the family members who post him for a while. Our guide to [stopping checking his Instagram](/blog/articles/stop-checking-ex-instagram/) goes through how.

## Why Does Having a Line Ready Help?

Because you only have to think once. Without one, every question makes you tell the story again, with a lump in your throat.

In one study, recently separated young adults who reflected on their breakup several times over nine weeks rebuilt a clearer sense of who they were, and that went along with less loneliness and fewer intrusive thoughts about the breakup ([Larson and Sbarra, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550614563085)). Writing your own short version of what happened is a small step in that direction.

## FAQ: What to Say When Family Asks About Your Breakup

**How do I tell my family I broke up?**
Tell your parents or one close relative before any big gathering, ideally by phone. Keep it to a few sentences, say how you're doing, and ask them to pass the news on so you don't have to.

**What do I say when people ask about my ex at Thanksgiving?**
One short sentence, then a new topic: "We broke up. I'm doing okay. How's school going?" If they push, repeat the same words instead of adding detail.

**Do I have to tell people why we broke up?**
No. "It didn't work out" is a complete answer. You can share more with the people you trust, when you want to.

**How do I stop my family passing on news about my ex?**
Ask them directly before the holidays: "Please don't tell me anything about him, even if I ask." If something slips out, say "I'd rather not know" and move on.
