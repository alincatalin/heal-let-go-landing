It's 3am and your heart is pounding. Your chest is tight. Your mind is running through every way this could have gone differently, every way the future could go wrong, and whether they're already with someone else.

You're not just sad. You're anxious. And nobody warned you a breakup could feel like this.

## Is Anxiety After a Breakup Normal?

**Yes, very.** Breakups often bring anxiety alongside sadness: a racing heart, a churning stomach, restlessness, trouble sleeping and a mind that won't stop. In a study of 192 university students after a breakup, those most distressed had more intrusive thoughts, more sleep problems and higher anxiety scores. They were also more likely to have been left, to have found the breakup sudden, and to feel rejected or betrayed ([Field et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20432597/)).

| What you might feel | What's going on |
| --- | --- |
| Racing heart, tight chest, shaky | Your stress response is switched on, as if you're under threat |
| Can't stop thinking, what-ifs | Your mind is trying to solve or undo the loss |
| Waking at 3 or 4am | Stress and quiet nights make thoughts louder |
| No appetite, nausea | Stress shuts down digestion |
| Urge to check your phone or their profile | Seeking reassurance, like a craving |
| Fear you'll never be okay, or never find anyone | Anxiety projects today's pain into the future |
| Panic attacks | A sudden peak of all of the above |

## Why Does a Breakup Cause Anxiety?

**Your brain reads the loss as a threat.** Rejection overlaps with pain processing: in a brain-scan study, looking at an ex who had broken up with you activated some of the same regions as physical pain ([Kross et al., 2011](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1102693108)). And rejection activates reward and craving systems linked to addiction ([Fisher et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00784.2009)). Part of breakup anxiety is withdrawal.

**Uncertainty feeds it.** Why did it end? Are they okay? Will they come back? Are they seeing someone? Anxiety thrives on questions you can't answer.

**Your attachment style matters.** In a survey of more than 5,000 people, those higher in attachment anxiety reported more preoccupation with the ex, more distress, more attempts to get back together, and more use of alcohol or drugs to cope ([Davis et al., 2003](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167203252884)). If you've always worried about being left, a breakup can hit that fear directly.

**Your routines just disappeared.** The person you texted all day, the plans, the predictable evenings. Without them, the nervous system has nothing to settle into.

## How to Calm Breakup Anxiety in the Moment

When the anxiety spikes, use this sequence. It takes about five minutes.

1. **Accept it.** Tell yourself: "This is anxiety. It's horrible, but it will pass." Fighting it tends to make it louder.
2. **Breathe slowly.** Breathe in while counting to five, out while counting to five. Mind, the UK mental health charity, suggests this as part of "ABC": accept, breathe, connect to your senses ([Mind](https://www.mind.org.uk/information-support/types-of-mental-health-problems/panic-attacks/)).
3. **Ground yourself in your senses.** Name five things you can see, four you can touch, three you can hear. Hold something cold. Stamp your feet.
4. **Get the spiral out of your head.** Write down exactly what you're afraid of or what you want to say to them. Our free [unsent text tool](/tools/unsent-text/) lets you type it in a chat that never sends. Heal's [SOS](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6754834593) walks you through the same steps on your phone.
5. **Don't act on the urge.** Not texting them, not checking their profile, not calling a mutual friend for news. Reassurance-seeking calms you for a minute and makes the next wave stronger.

## Panic Attacks After a Breakup

A panic attack is a sudden rush of intense fear with strong physical symptoms: racing heartbeat, shortness of breath, chest pain, trembling, dizziness, nausea, or a feeling of dread or unreality. According to the NHS, most panic attacks last between 5 and 20 minutes, and although they're frightening, they're not dangerous ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/panic-disorder/)).

During one:

- Stay where you are if you can
- Breathe slowly and deeply
- Remind yourself the attack will pass
- Focus on something calm: a picture, a sound, a texture

If you have chest pain or trouble breathing and you're not sure it's panic, get medical help right away. If panic attacks keep happening, or you're avoiding places because you're afraid of having one, talk to a doctor. Talking therapies work well for panic.

## How to Reduce Anxiety Over the Next Few Weeks

**Go no contact.** Every message, reply or story check reopens the uncertainty. Contact with an ex is linked to slower recovery ([Sbarra and Emery, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1350-4126.2005.00112.x)). Our [no contact rule guide](/blog/articles/no-contact-rule-guide/) explains how, and [should I block my ex](/blog/articles/should-i-block-my-ex/) helps if muting isn't enough.

**Protect your sleep.** Same bedtime, phone out of reach, no scrolling their profile in bed. Anxiety is worse when you're exhausted. Our guide on [why breakups hurt more at night](/blog/articles/why-breakups-hurt-more-at-night/) has a night plan.

**Move your body.** Physical activity burns off stress hormones. A brisk walk is enough.

**Cut caffeine and alcohol.** Both can make anxiety and sleep worse.

**Give your worries a time slot.** Fifteen minutes in the early evening to write every worry down. When one shows up at another time, tell it "later".

**Rebuild some routine.** Regular meals, a morning walk, a standing call with a friend. Predictability calms the nervous system.

**Write it out.** In a randomized study, students who wrote about their feelings after a breakup avoided the rise in tension and physical symptoms that a comparison group had ([Lepore and Greenberg, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1080/08870440290025768)).

## When to Get Help for Breakup Anxiety

Talk to a doctor or therapist if:

- Anxiety is there most days for several weeks and isn't easing
- You're having repeated panic attacks
- You can't sleep, eat or work
- You're using alcohol or drugs to calm down
- You're also feeling very low or hopeless. See [depression after a breakup](/blog/articles/depression-after-a-breakup/)

If you have any thoughts of harming yourself, call or text 988 in the US, or find a free line for your country at [findahelpline.com](https://findahelpline.com).

## FAQ: Anxiety After a Breakup

**How long does anxiety last after a breakup?**
It's usually worst in the first weeks and eases as routines return and contact stops. If it's still intense after a month or two, or getting worse, get support.

**Why do I have chest pain after a breakup?**
Stress and anxiety can cause chest tightness, and emotional pain shares brain pathways with physical pain. But chest pain always deserves a check if it's severe, crushing or comes with breathlessness. Read about broken heart syndrome in [how to heal a broken heart](/blog/articles/how-to-heal-a-broken-heart/).

**Why am I so anxious even though I ended it?**
Ending it doesn't stop the loss. You're grieving, facing uncertainty and changing routines, and you may doubt the decision. All of that can cause anxiety.

**Why do I feel anxious when I think my ex is seeing someone?**
That thought hits the fear of being replaced. Checking to find out usually makes it worse. Mute them and ask friends not to pass on news.

**Can a breakup cause panic attacks?**
Yes. Intense stress can trigger panic attacks, even in people who have never had one. They're frightening but not dangerous. If they keep happening, talk to a doctor.

**What helps breakup anxiety at night?**
A wind-down routine, phone out of reach, slow breathing, and writing down what's spinning in your head. If the urge to text them comes with it, see [what to do instead of texting your ex](/blog/articles/what-to-do-instead-of-texting-your-ex/).
