If you're here, you probably can't point to the day it ended. Maybe the texts got slower. Maybe they started seeing someone else and you found out on Instagram. Maybe you're technically still talking and you already know it's over.

And on top of the hurt, there's a second feeling: that you're not allowed to be this upset. You were never official. What are you even grieving?

You're grieving something real. Let's start there.

## Why Does a Situationship Hurt So Much When It "Wasn't Even Real"?

It was real to you. You knew how they took their coffee, and you grabbed your phone every time it buzzed in case it was them. There was no label, but you were attached all the same.

A situationship can also hurt in ways a normal breakup doesn't:

- There's no clear ending, so your brain keeps the file open.
- You never got to say "I want more," or you said it and got a shrug.
- Friends may not take it seriously ("you weren't even together").
- You spent months reading signals instead of getting answers.

That last one matters more than it sounds. In a small study, women looked at the profiles of men who had supposedly rated them. Women who didn't know whether the men liked them a lot or only a bit thought about those men more, and felt more drawn to them, than women who were told the men liked them best ([Whitchurch, Wilson and Gilbert, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797610393745)). It was one short experiment with strangers, so we can't stretch it too far. But it puts a name to something you may know: not knowing where you stand keeps someone on your mind.

A situationship can be months of exactly that.

## How Is a Situationship Ending Different From a Breakup?

A breakup has a day, a conversation and usually some sympathy. A situationship often has none of those, which changes what getting over it takes.

| | Breakup | Situationship ending |
|---|---|---|
| How it ends | A conversation, a date you can point to | Slower replies, a fade, or finding out they're seeing someone |
| What you're left with | Grief, but a clear "it's over" | Grief plus "was it even anything?" |
| What friends say | "I'm so sorry" | Sometimes "you weren't even together" |
| What helps first | Distance and time | Deciding it's over yourself, then distance and time |

## Am I Allowed to Grieve a Relationship That Never Had a Label?

Yes.

There's a name for grief that other people don't recognize or don't think counts. The grief researcher Kenneth Doka called it disenfranchised grief in a 1989 book of the same name (Doka, 1989). The examples usually given include ex-partners and former boyfriends or girlfriends, people whose loss others tend not to count. When nobody around you treats the loss as a loss, you tend to hide it, and hidden grief is harder to move through.

So here's your permission, if you need it from someone. You can cry about a person you were "just talking to." You can take a sad day. You can tell a friend, "I know we weren't official, but I'm really hurt," and ask them to treat it like a breakup. Most good friends will, once you say it plainly.

## Why Can't I Stop Thinking About Them?

Because nothing told your brain it was over.

The family therapist Pauline Boss spent her career studying what she called ambiguous loss: losses with no clear ending, where someone is gone in one way but present in another (Boss, 1999). Her work was mostly about families, not dating. But the core idea fits a situationship well. Without a clear ending, people find it hard to start grieving, because some part of them is still waiting.

That's what the 1am replays are. Your mind is still looking for the answer you never got. Did they like me? Was I imagining it? If I'd been more chill, would it have worked?

You may never get those answers from them. What you can do is stop waiting for them, and that starts with deciding it's over.

## How Do I End a Situationship That Never Officially Started?

You decide. You don't need their agreement or a big talk.

That might mean telling them. If you want to send something, keep it short and don't ask a question that needs an answer:

> Hey, I've realized I want something that this isn't. I'm going to step back. I hope you're well.

> I liked you a lot, and I don't want to keep doing the in-between. Take care of yourself.

Or it might mean saying nothing at all, especially if they're the one who faded. You're allowed to end it in your own head and simply stop replying. ## What If They Keep Coming Back?

They might. Situationships are famous for the "hey stranger" text three weeks later, or the story view at 2am, or the "I was just thinking about you" that goes nowhere.

Each of those restarts the guessing. One message can undo a week of feeling better, because it reopens the question you were finally putting down.

What helps:

- Mute or archive the chat so their name isn't the first thing you see.
- Mute their stories. If you keep checking their profile anyway, our guide on [how to stop checking your ex's Instagram](/blog/articles/stop-checking-ex-instagram/) is written for exactly that loop.
- Decide in advance what you'll do if they text. "I'll wait 24 hours and talk to a friend before I reply" is a good rule.
- If they want more now, make them say it clearly. Vague interest is how you got here.

## How Do I Get Over a Situationship? A Plan for the First Few Weeks

Here's what we'd suggest, kept small on purpose.

**This week:** Decide it's over and say so to one friend. Mute the chat. Delete the dating app if that's where they keep reappearing. Eat, sleep, drink water. Seriously.

**Week two:** Give the thinking a slot. Twenty minutes in the evening to replay it, cry or write. When they come into your head at other times, tell yourself "later." This is easier than trying not to think about them at all.

**Week three:** Write down what you actually wanted. Not from them, from a relationship. Consistency? Someone who actually texts back? That list is useful later, when someone new shows up and you're tempted to settle for "maybe" again.

**Week four onwards:** Add one thing to your week that has nothing to do with dating. A pottery class, or a standing Tuesday dinner with a friend. Something that's yours.

## How Long Does It Take to Get Over a Situationship?

There's no fixed time. Some people feel lighter in a couple of weeks. For others, especially after months of hoping, it takes longer than a "real" breakup would, because there was so much uncertainty to untangle.

You'll know it's working when you notice you went a whole day without checking whether they've watched your story. Then two days. For most people it happens like that, slowly, and you only see it looking back.

## FAQ: Getting Over a Situationship

**What is a situationship?**
A situationship is a romantic or sexual connection that never gets a clear label or commitment. You act like more than friends, but you never agree on what you are, which is why it can end without a breakup and still hurt like one.

**Why is a situationship so hard to get over?**
Because there's usually no clear ending, and uncertainty keeps people on your mind. You may also feel like you're not allowed to grieve, which makes it harder to talk about and move through.

**Is it normal to be this upset over a situationship?**
Yes. The attachment can be just as real as in an official relationship. Grief that others don't recognize is common enough that researchers have a name for it: disenfranchised grief.

**Should I tell them I'm ending the situationship?**
You can, with a short message that doesn't ask for anything back. You can also end it without saying a word, especially if they've already faded. Either is fine.

**What if they text me after I've moved on?**
You don't have to reply. If you do want to respond, wait a day and talk to a friend first. If they want something more now, they can say it clearly.

**How long does it take to get over a situationship?**
It varies. Some people feel better within weeks, others take months. It tends to get easier once you stop waiting for an answer from them and decide it's over yourself.
